# Pie Charts and Bar Charts: Picturing Categories

In order to visualize the numerical summaries we've obtained, we need a graphical display. There are two simple graphical displays for visualizing the distribution of categorical data:

1. The Pie Chart
2. The Bar Chart

Here is the distribution of the Body Image variable displayed first as a pie chart and then as a bar chart:

```{figure} images/gen/m04-body-image-pie.svg
:alt: A pie chart of the body image responses of 1,200 U.S. college students. The About right slice takes up 71.3% of the pie (855 students), Overweight takes 19.6% (235 students), and Underweight takes 9.2% (110 students).
```

```{figure} images/gen/m04-body-image-bar.svg
:alt: A bar chart of the same body image data, with percent on the vertical axis. The About right bar reaches 71.3%, the Overweight bar 19.6%, and the Underweight bar 9.2%.
```

Note that the pie chart emphasizes how each category relates to the whole (all 1,200 students), while the bar chart makes it easier to compare the categories to one another. Either display is appropriate for a single categorical variable.

## Check Your Understanding: Summarizing a Categorical Variable

:::{quiz} Using the displays above, what percentage of the sampled students felt that their weight was NOT about right?
:hint: Combine the categories other than "About right"—or subtract from 100%.
:feedback-0: 19.6% is only the Overweight category; don't forget Underweight.
:feedback-1: Correct! $19.6% + 9.2% = 28.8%$ (or equivalently $100% - 71.3%$, up to rounding).
:feedback-2: 9.2% is only the Underweight category; don't forget Overweight.
:feedback-3: 71.3% is the percentage who felt about right.

* 71.3%
:::

Now that we have summarized the distribution of values in the Body Image variable, let's go back and interpret the results in the context of the questions that we posed:

:::{quiz} Which of the following best describes how the sampled students are divided across the three body image categories?
:hint: Look at the relative sizes of the three categories in either display.
:feedback-0: The students are far from equally divided—one category holds almost three-quarters of the responses.
:feedback-1: Correct! A large majority (about 70%) feel about right, about 20% feel overweight, and about 10% feel underweight—roughly a 7:2:1 pattern.
:feedback-2: It's the reverse: far more students feel overweight than underweight.
* The students are roughly equally divided across the three categories
* *Most students (about 70%) feel about right; among the rest, about twice as many feel overweight as underweight
* Most students feel about right, and more students feel underweight than overweight
:::

Now that we've interpreted the results, there are some other interesting questions that arise:

- Can we reliably generalize our results to the entire population of interest and conclude that a similar distribution across body image categories exists among all U.S. college students? In particular, can we make such a generalization even though our sample consisted of only 1,200 students, which is a very small fraction of the entire population?
- If we had separated our sample by gender and looked at males and females separately, would we have found a similar distribution across body image categories?

These are the types of questions that we will deal with in future sections of the course.
