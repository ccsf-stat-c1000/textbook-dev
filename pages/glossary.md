# Glossary

Definitions of the key terms used throughout this book. Terms are listed alphabetically. Wherever a term is introduced in the text it links back to this page, and you can hover over any linked term to see its definition without leaving the page.

```{glossary}
alternative hypothesis
: The claim that the researcher is gathering evidence *for*, written $H_a$. It states that the parameter differs from the value in the null hypothesis, in a specified direction (one-sided) or in either direction (two-sided).

analysis of variance
: A hypothesis test, also called ANOVA, used when the explanatory variable is categorical with more than two groups and the response variable is quantitative. It compares the variation *between* group means to the variation *within* groups.

association
: A relationship between two variables in which knowing the value of one tells you something about the likely value of the other. Association does not by itself establish causation.

bar chart
: A graph for a single categorical variable in which the height of each bar shows the count or percentage of individuals in that category.

Bayes' rule
: A formula for reversing the direction of a conditional probability, computing $P(A \mid B)$ from $P(B \mid A)$ and the marginal probabilities.

bias
: A systematic tendency for a sampling method or measurement process to produce values that are too high or too low. Bias is an error in the *process*, not a matter of chance, so it does not shrink as the sample gets larger.

bimodal
: A distribution with two distinct peaks, often a sign that the data combine two different groups.

binomial random variable
: A discrete random variable that counts the number of successes in a fixed number of independent trials, each with the same probability of success.

blinding
: Withholding knowledge of which treatment a participant received. In a single-blind study the participants do not know; in a double-blind study neither the participants nor the people assessing the outcome know.

block design
: An experimental design in which participants are first divided into groups (blocks) that are similar with respect to some variable, and then randomized to treatments separately within each block.

boxplot
: A display built from the five-number summary. The box spans the first to third quartile with the median marked inside, and whiskers extend to the most extreme values that are not outliers.

categorical variable
: A variable that records which of several groups or categories an individual belongs to, such as eye color or treatment group. Also called a qualitative variable.

causation
: A relationship in which changing the explanatory variable actually produces a change in the response variable. Causation can be established by a randomized controlled experiment, but generally not by an observational study.

census
: An attempt to collect data on every individual in the population, rather than on a sample.

center
: A value that describes where the bulk of a distribution lies, usually reported as the mean or the median.

central limit theorem
: The result that the sampling distribution of the sample mean becomes approximately normal as the sample size grows, whatever the shape of the population distribution. This is what makes normal-based inference so widely applicable.

chi-square test of independence
: A hypothesis test for a relationship between two categorical variables, based on comparing the observed counts in a two-way table to the counts expected if the variables were independent.

cluster sampling
: A sampling method in which the population is divided into groups (clusters), a random sample of clusters is selected, and every individual in the chosen clusters is included.

complement
: For an event $A$, the event that $A$ does *not* occur, written $A^c$. Its probability is $1 - P(A)$.

conditional probability
: The probability that one event occurs given that another event has occurred, written $P(A \mid B)$.

confidence interval
: An interval of plausible values for a population parameter, computed from sample data. It takes the form *estimate $\pm$ margin of error*.

confidence level
: The long-run percentage of confidence intervals, built by the same method from repeated samples, that would capture the true parameter. Common levels are 90%, 95%, and 99%.

confounding variable
: A variable that is associated with both the explanatory and the response variable, making it impossible to tell which one is responsible for the observed relationship.

continuous random variable
: A random variable that can take any value in an interval. Its probabilities are found as areas under a density curve rather than by adding up individual outcomes.

control group
: The group in an experiment that does not receive the treatment of interest, providing a baseline for comparison.

convenience sample
: A sample made up of whichever individuals are easiest to reach. It is prone to bias and does not support generalization to the population.

correlation coefficient
: A number, written $r$, that measures the direction and strength of the *linear* relationship between two quantitative variables. It ranges from $-1$ to $1$ and has no units.

critical value
: The multiplier used in a confidence interval, determined by the confidence level and the relevant distribution, such as $z^*$ or $t^*$.

degrees of freedom
: A parameter that determines which member of a family of distributions (such as $t$, chi-square, or $F$) applies in a given situation. It is generally tied to the sample size.

density curve
: A smooth curve that describes the distribution of a continuous random variable. The total area under the curve is 1, and probabilities correspond to areas.

dependent events
: Two events for which knowing that one occurred changes the probability of the other.

descriptive statistics
: Methods for summarizing and displaying the data actually collected, without attempting to draw conclusions beyond them.

deviation
: The distance of an observation from the mean, $x - \bar{x}$. Deviations are the raw material of the standard deviation.

direction
: Whether the relationship between two quantitative variables is positive (both increase together), negative (one increases as the other decreases), or neither.

discrete random variable
: A random variable whose possible values can be listed, such as a count.

disjoint events
: Two events that cannot both occur on the same trial. Also called mutually exclusive events.

distribution
: A description of what values a variable takes and how often it takes them.

double-blind
: An experiment in which neither the participants nor the people measuring the response know who received which treatment.

empirical rule
: For a roughly normal distribution, the observation that about 68% of values fall within one standard deviation of the mean, about 95% within two, and about 99.7% within three. Also called the standard deviation rule.

event
: Any collection of outcomes from a random experiment.

expected value
: The long-run average value of a random variable over many repetitions, written $E(X)$ or $\mu$. Also called the mean of the random variable.

experiment
: A study in which the researcher assigns the values of the explanatory variable to the participants, rather than merely observing them. Only an experiment with randomized assignment can establish causation.

explanatory variable
: The variable thought to explain or predict changes in another variable. Also called the independent or predictor variable.

exploratory data analysis
: The process of summarizing, visualizing, and examining data in order to understand its main features before any formal inference.

extrapolation
: Using a regression line to predict a response for an explanatory value outside the range of the observed data. The prediction is unreliable because there is no evidence the pattern continues.

five-number summary
: The minimum, first quartile, median, third quartile, and maximum of a distribution.

form
: The shape of the overall pattern in a scatterplot, such as linear or curved.

frequency
: The count of how many individuals fall into a given category or class.

generalizability
: The extent to which conclusions drawn from a sample can be extended to the larger population. It depends on how the sample was selected.

histogram
: A graph of the distribution of a quantitative variable in which the data are grouped into intervals and the height of each bar shows how many observations fall in that interval.

hypothesis testing
: A form of inference that assesses whether sample data provide enough evidence against a specified claim about a population parameter.

independent events
: Two events for which knowing that one occurred does not change the probability of the other.

individuals
: The people, animals, or objects described by a dataset. Also called cases or observational units.

inference
: The process of drawing conclusions about a population based on data from a sample, together with an assessment of how reliable those conclusions are.

intercept
: The predicted value of the response variable when the explanatory variable equals zero; the constant term in a regression equation.

interquartile range
: The distance between the first and third quartiles, $Q_3 - Q_1$. It measures the spread of the middle half of the data and is resistant to outliers.

intersection
: The event that two events both occur, written $A \text{ and } B$.

interval estimate
: A range of plausible values for a parameter, as opposed to a single number. A confidence interval is the standard form.

law of large numbers
: The result that the relative frequency of an outcome approaches its true probability as the number of repetitions grows.

least squares regression line
: The line that minimizes the sum of the squared vertical distances between the observed responses and the line. It is the standard summary of a linear relationship.

lurking variable
: A variable that is not among the variables being studied but that affects the relationship between them.

margin of error
: The amount added to and subtracted from a point estimate to form a confidence interval. It reflects both the confidence level and the variability of the estimate.

matched pairs design
: A design in which observations come in naturally linked pairs, such as before-and-after measurements on the same person, so that the analysis is carried out on the differences within pairs.

mean
: The arithmetic average of a set of values, $\bar{x}$ for a sample and $\mu$ for a population. It is sensitive to outliers.

median
: The middle value of an ordered dataset; half the observations fall below it. It is resistant to outliers.

mode
: A peak in a distribution, or the most frequently occurring value.

multiplication rule
: The rule for finding the probability that two events both occur: $P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \cdot P(B \mid A)$. When the events are independent this simplifies to $P(A) \cdot P(B)$.

multistage sampling
: A sampling method that applies several sampling techniques in sequence, such as selecting clusters and then sampling within them.

nonresponse
: Failure to obtain data from individuals selected for the sample, which can bias the results if non-responders differ systematically from responders.

normal distribution
: A symmetric, bell-shaped continuous distribution described by its mean and standard deviation. It is central to statistics because so many sampling distributions are approximately normal.

null hypothesis
: The claim being tested, written $H_0$, usually stating that there is no effect or no difference. A hypothesis test asks whether the data provide evidence against it.

observational study
: A study in which the researcher records values of variables without assigning treatments. It can establish association but generally not causation.

outlier
: An observation that falls far outside the overall pattern of the data.

p-value
: The probability, computed assuming the null hypothesis is true, of getting data at least as extreme as what was actually observed. A small p-value is evidence against the null hypothesis.

parameter
: A number that describes a population, such as $\mu$ or $p$. Its value is usually unknown.

pie chart
: A circular graph for a single categorical variable in which each slice represents a category's share of the whole.

placebo
: An inert treatment that resembles the real one, given so that participants in the control group have the same experience apart from the active ingredient.

placebo effect
: An improvement in a participant's condition that results from the expectation of treatment rather than the treatment itself.

point estimate
: A single number computed from sample data and used as a best guess for a population parameter.

pooled
: Combined across samples. A pooled estimate uses data from two or more groups to produce a single, more precise estimate of a quantity assumed to be common to them.

population
: The entire group of individuals about whom we want to draw conclusions.

power
: The probability that a hypothesis test correctly rejects a false null hypothesis. Power increases with sample size and with the size of the true effect.

probability
: A number between 0 and 1 describing how likely an outcome is, understood as the long-run relative frequency with which it occurs.

probability distribution
: A description of all the values a random variable can take together with their probabilities.

prospective study
: An observational study that identifies participants first and then follows them forward in time to record outcomes.

quantitative variable
: A variable that takes numerical values for which arithmetic makes sense, such as height or income.

quartile
: One of the three values that divide an ordered dataset into four equal parts. The first quartile $Q_1$ is the 25th percentile and the third quartile $Q_3$ is the 75th.

random variable
: A numerical quantity whose value is determined by the outcome of a random experiment.

randomization
: The use of chance to assign participants to treatment groups, which balances both known and unknown variables across groups and is what allows an experiment to support causal conclusions.

range
: The distance between the largest and smallest values in a dataset.

relative frequency
: The proportion or percentage of individuals in a category, rather than the raw count.

residual
: The difference between an observed response value and the value predicted by the regression line.

resistant measure
: A summary that is not strongly affected by outliers or extreme values. The median and interquartile range are resistant; the mean and standard deviation are not.

response bias
: A distortion introduced by the way data are collected from participants, such as leading question wording or reluctance to answer honestly.

response variable
: The variable whose changes are being explained or predicted. Also called the dependent or outcome variable.

retrospective study
: An observational study that identifies participants after the outcome has occurred and looks backward at their history.

sample
: The subset of the population from which data are actually collected.

sample mean
: The average of the values in a sample, $\bar{x}$, used to estimate the population mean.

sample proportion
: The fraction of individuals in a sample with a characteristic of interest, $\hat{p}$, used to estimate the population proportion.

sample space
: The set of all possible outcomes of a random experiment.

sampling bias
: Bias arising because the method of choosing the sample systematically favors certain members of the population.

sampling distribution
: The distribution of a statistic across all possible samples of a given size from the population. It describes how much the statistic varies from sample to sample.

sampling frame
: The list of individuals from which the sample is actually drawn. If it does not match the population, undercoverage results.

scatterplot
: A graph of the relationship between two quantitative variables, with the explanatory variable on the horizontal axis and the response variable on the vertical axis.

sensitivity
: For a diagnostic test, the probability of a positive result given that the condition is present.

shape
: The overall form of a distribution, described in terms of symmetry, skewness, and the number of peaks.

significance level
: The threshold, written $\alpha$, below which a p-value is taken as sufficient evidence against the null hypothesis. It is chosen before the data are examined; 0.05 is conventional.

simple random sample
: A sample chosen so that every group of the given size has an equal chance of being selected. Often abbreviated SRS.

Simpson's paradox
: A situation in which an association observed in aggregated data reverses direction when the data are broken out by a third variable.

skewed left
: A distribution with a longer tail on the low side, so that the mean is generally pulled below the median.

skewed right
: A distribution with a longer tail on the high side, so that the mean is generally pulled above the median.

slope
: In a regression equation, the predicted change in the response variable for a one-unit increase in the explanatory variable.

specificity
: For a diagnostic test, the probability of a negative result given that the condition is absent.

spread
: How much the values in a distribution vary, usually measured by the range, interquartile range, or standard deviation.

standard deviation
: A measure of spread describing the typical distance of observations from the mean, written $s$ for a sample and $\sigma$ for a population.

standard error
: The standard deviation of a sampling distribution; it measures the typical amount by which a statistic varies from sample to sample.

standard normal distribution
: The normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 1, obtained by converting values to z-scores.

statistic
: A number computed from sample data, such as $\bar{x}$ or $\hat{p}$, used to estimate a parameter.

statistically significant
: Describing a result whose p-value falls below the chosen significance level, meaning it would be unlikely to arise by chance alone if the null hypothesis were true.

stemplot
: A display of a quantitative distribution in which each value is split into a leading digit (the stem) and a trailing digit (the leaf), preserving the individual data values.

stratified sampling
: A sampling method in which the population is divided into groups (strata) that are similar within themselves, and a random sample is drawn from each stratum.

strength
: How closely the points in a scatterplot follow the overall form of the relationship.

symmetric
: A distribution whose left and right halves are approximate mirror images.

t-distribution
: A family of symmetric distributions, indexed by degrees of freedom, used in place of the normal distribution when the population standard deviation is unknown and must be estimated from the data.

test statistic
: A number computed from sample data that measures how far the data fall from what the null hypothesis predicts, expressed in standard error units.

treatment
: A specific condition applied to participants in an experiment.

tree diagram
: A branching diagram that lays out the possible sequences of outcomes in a multi-stage random process along with their probabilities.

two-way table
: A table that displays the counts for every combination of categories of two categorical variables.

Type I error
: Rejecting a null hypothesis that is actually true; a false positive. Its probability is the significance level $\alpha$.

Type II error
: Failing to reject a null hypothesis that is actually false; a false negative.

undercoverage
: The exclusion of part of the population from the sampling frame, so that those individuals have no chance of being selected.

unimodal
: A distribution with a single peak.

union
: The event that at least one of two events occurs, written $A \text{ or } B$.

variable
: Any characteristic recorded about the individuals in a dataset.

variance
: The square of the standard deviation; the average of the squared deviations from the mean.

volunteer sample
: A sample made up of people who select themselves by responding to a general appeal. It typically over-represents those with strong opinions.

z-score
: The number of standard deviations an observation falls above or below the mean, computed as $(x - \mu)/\sigma$. Also called a standardized score.
```
